The corpus is the product

An assistant that answers from policy can only be as right, as current and as discreet as the store it reads. Part 1 · covers the clause as the unit of retrieval and citation

A clause, not a document

6 min live

A forty-page expenses policy cannot be cited usefully, and it cannot be permissioned finer than "all of it". One clause can: it answers one kind of question, it has one version history, and one person owns it. So the unit Tidewell stores, retrieves and cites is the clause, and every field on it is there because a later step reads it.

Tidewell's constructed store holds 49 clause versions across 16 documents: 42 current and 7 superseded. Of the current ones, 33 are readable by all staff and 9 are restricted. The bench in session 8 prints the same counts from the same file.

LiveHow big is a clause?3 min▶

Big enough to answer one kind of question on its own, small enough that a citation points at something a person can read in ten seconds. Tidewell's clauses average 21.76 tokens, title included, once stopwords are removed. That is a choice about this corpus, not a rule: policy text is already written in numbered clauses, so the authors have done the chunking. For documents that are not, chunking strategies and their trade-offs belong to the RAG course's chunking session, and this track does not reteach them.

Real world

Why the store, not the model, is the asset. The paper that named retrieval-augmented generation, Lewis and colleagues in 2020, described it as combining "parametric and non-parametric memory", and showed that knowledge can be updated by replacing the retrieved index rather than retraining the model. For a policy assistant that is the whole design: when HR changes the leave policy, you change the store. The same paper calls providing provenance an open problem, which is why the citation and its metadata are built here, not assumed.

Part 2 · covers keeping superseded versions without letting them answer

Old rules stay in the store

6 min live

Deleting the old leave policy feels tidy and is wrong. HR will be asked what the policy said on the day an employee booked their leave, and the answer is the old version. So old versions stay, flagged superseded, and the assistant is never allowed to answer from them. Two facts make the flag matter more than it looks.

LiveStaff ask in the words of the version they learned3 min▶

Tidewell renamed "holiday entitlement" to "annual leave", "per diem" to "daily allowance" and "remote work" to "working from home". Staff did not. Seven of the 54 golden questions in session 3 use the old vocabulary, and on the bench in session 8 a retriever without the version filter cites a superseded clause for five of them. Keyword retrieval rewards the old wording, because the old clause literally contains the words in the question.

The fix is not to delete the old clause or to rewrite the question. It is a filter that removes superseded versions before ranking, built in session 7, and new text that bridges the vocabulary: Tidewell's current leave clause opens "Annual leave, formerly called holiday entitlement".

Self-studyTwo current versions of one rule2 min▶

The quietest fault in a policy store is a new version loaded without the old one being flagged. Both say "current", both get retrieved, and the assistant's answer depends on which one BM25 happens to rank first for that question's wording. Session 8's break button builds exactly this fault on the expenses policy and shows the go/no-go passing it. The validator below catches it at load time, which is cheaper than catching it at launch.

Part 3 · covers access written on every clause

Who may read it is part of the clause

5 min live

Most of a policy store is for everyone. The rest is the reason the permission filter exists, and it has to be written on the clause by the person who owns it, not inferred later from which folder the document came from.

LiveFive groups, and the rule that reads them3 min▶

Every clause names exactly one access group: all (everyone), managers (managers and HR), hr, finance or it. Every asker belongs to one group: staff, manager, hr, finance or it. A clause may be read when the asker's group is in the clause's list. That is the whole rule, and it is deliberately boring: session 5 runs it inside retrieval, before anything is ranked, and the bench counts every question whose retrieved set breaks it. Richer models, roles, attributes and row-level rules, belong to the data access control course.

Self-studyWhy the owner sets the access, not the folder2 min▶

Access inherited from a shared drive folder is access nobody decided. One HR folder at Tidewell holds the public leave policy and the salary bands side by side; inherit the folder and both are "HR". OWASP's Top 10 for LLM applications (2025) asks for "fine-grained access controls and permission-aware vector and embedding stores" under LLM08, and limiting sensitive data "based on the principle of least privilege" under LLM02. Fine-grained means per clause, written by the person who can say why.

Build-along

A validator that refuses a bad store

★ 20 min · everyone builds

Plain Python, no libraries. Four constructed clauses, three of them with a fault planted on purpose, and a check that must find all three. Run it from an empty scratch folder, then point it at your own clause file.

Write four clauses as plain records. Two versions of the leave rule, correctly flagged, and two of the meals rule with three faults planted: a blank owner on version 5, an unknown access group on version 4, and version 4 not flagged superseded.

★ validate.py · part 1, the clauses (constructed)clauses = [ {"key": "HR-LV-1", "version": "v3", "effective": "2026-01-01", "superseded": False, "owner": "HR policy lead", "access": "all", "title": "Annual leave", "text": "Full-time staff earn 20 days of annual leave each calendar year."}, {"key": "HR-LV-1", "version": "v2", "effective": "2023-04-01", "superseded": True, "owner": "HR policy lead", "access": "all", "title": "Holiday entitlement", "text": "Every employee receives 18 days holiday entitlement each year."}, {"key": "FIN-EXP-2", "version": "v5", "effective": "2026-02-01", "superseded": False, "owner": "", "access": "all", "title": "Meals when travelling", "text": "Meals are covered by a daily allowance of SGD 60 in Singapore."}, {"key": "FIN-EXP-2", "version": "v4", "effective": "2024-01-01", "superseded": False, "owner": "Financial controller", "access": "everyone", "title": "Per diem", "text": "Per diem for meals: SGD 50 per day in Singapore."}, ]

Gates one and two: every field present, and a known access group. An empty string counts as missing; an owner of "" is no owner.

★ validate.py · part 2, fields and groupsGROUPS = {"all", "managers", "hr", "finance", "it"} REQUIRED = ["key", "version", "effective", "superseded", "owner", "access", "title", "text"] problems = [] for c in clauses: cid = c["key"] + "@" + c["version"] for f in REQUIRED: if c.get(f) in (None, ""): problems.append(f"{cid}: missing {f}") if c["access"] not in GROUPS: problems.append(f"{cid}: unknown access group {c['access']!r}")

Gate three: exactly one current version per key. Group the current versions by key and complain about any key with more or fewer than one.

★ validate.py · part 3, one current versioncurrent = {} for c in clauses: if not c["superseded"]: current.setdefault(c["key"], []).append(c["version"]) for key, versions in sorted(current.items()): if len(versions) != 1: problems.append(f"{key}: {len(versions)} current versions {versions}") print(len(clauses), "clauses,", len(problems), "problems") for p in problems: print(" " + p)

Run it and read the three lines. python3 validate.py prints exactly this. If you see fewer than three problems, a gate is broken; if you see more, a clause has a fault you did not plant.

★ Output4 clauses, 3 problems FIN-EXP-2@v5: missing owner FIN-EXP-2@v4: unknown access group 'everyone' FIN-EXP-2: 2 current versions ['v5', 'v4']

Fix the store, not the validator. Give version 5 its owner, set version 4's access to "all" and its superseded flag to True, and run again: 4 clauses, 0 problems. Then make the validator the first step of every load, and fail the load on any problem.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Take one real policy document from your organisation and split it into clauses. Fill the eight fields for each; leave a field blank rather than guess.
  2. Find a policy that changed in the last two years. Write both versions as clauses, flag the old one, and note which words staff still use from it.
  3. For every clause in your sample, write the access group and the name of the person who decided it. Count how many you could not name.
  4. Add a fourth gate to the validator: an effective date in the future, or a superseded version with a later effective date than the current one.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Why does Tidewell store and cite clauses rather than whole documents?

The clause is the smallest unit that can be cited usefully and permissioned finely; the salary bands and the pay-day rule live in one pay policy but have different readers. B is a claim about chunking that depends on the corpus, which the RAG course covers.

2 · HR replaced the leave policy. What should happen to the old version in the store?

Somebody will need to know what the rule said on a given day, so the old version stays. C is exactly the fault that lets "holiday entitlement" questions get the old 18 days. A loses the record; D makes both rules wrong.

3 · The validator reports "FIN-EXP-2: 2 current versions". Which later failure does this gate prevent?

With two versions marked current, the version filter keeps both, and which one answers depends on the question's wording. Session 8's break button builds this on the same rule and the go/no-go passes it. Access faults are gate two's job.

Ship It: Internal AI Assistant · Leader session 4 of 6

What it says when it doesn't know

By the end of session 3, Tidewell Freight's assistant (a constructed 1,200-person freight forwarder) leaks nothing and cites only current policy. It answers 97.6% of the answerable questions correctly, and the go/no-go board still says NO-GO. The reason is the other twelve questions: the ones it should not answer at all. Tonight is about what the assistant says then, what it opens, and where the line sits that makes it stop. You leave with a hand-off message, a ticket template and a threshold decision note.

Part 1 · covers why a highly accurate assistant can still be a no-go

Confident and wrong

6 min live

Rung 3 of the bench is the assistant most demos show: permission filter on, superseded versions out, top three clauses retrieved, an answer every time. Its accuracy on the answerable questions is the best on the ladder. Its behaviour on the other twelve is the reason it does not ship.

LiveRead the two cards row by row3 min▶

Tidewell's go/no-go policy (its own choice, not a standard) asks for correct answers on at least 70% of the answerable questions, right hand-offs on at least 80% of the twelve it should not answer, and confident wrong answers on at most 5% of high-risk, 10% of medium-risk and 20% of low-risk questions. Rung 3 clears the accuracy bar with room to spare at 41 of 42. It fails three other lines at once, and the bench prints all three reasons: medium-risk 31.6% over 10.0%, low-risk 35.7% over 20.0%, hands off 8.3% where 80.0% is needed.

Rung 4 changes nothing about retrieval. It adds one rule: if the best clause scores below 3.0, do not answer; say so and open a ticket. Correct answers fall to 38 of 42 (90.5%), right hand-offs rise to 11 of 12 (91.7%), and the board says GO.

LiveWhy "accuracy" hides the failure3 min▶

Accuracy is measured on the questions that have an answer. The dangerous questions are the ones that do not: the six restricted ones, where a clause exists but the asker may not read it, and the six with no policy anywhere. A ranking of clauses always has a first place, so an assistant without a hand-off rule answers all of them, citing a real clause that does not answer the question. That is why a single accuracy number on a vendor slide tells you nothing about whether to ship.

NIST's Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1) names the risk confabulation: "the production of confidently stated but erroneous or false content". OWASP's Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 names the human side under LLM09 Misinformation: overreliance, when users "place excessive trust in LLM-generated content". An internal assistant invites both, because staff are inclined to trust anything that quotes their own company's policy.

Self-studyGrounding reduces this, it does not remove it2 min▶

Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI (IMDA and AI Verify Foundation, 2024) lists retrieval-augmented generation among techniques "commonly used to reduce hallucinations and improve accuracy". Rung 3 is a fully grounded assistant, and it still produces confident wrong answers on 31.6% of medium-risk questions, because grounding constrains what it answers from, not whether it should answer. Only a hand-off rule does that. No hallucination rate is quoted here for any assistant or model: none read at source would transfer to your corpus, and Tidewell's numbers are a constructed demonstration of the mechanism.

Real world

Where NIST puts the harm. The Generative AI Profile does not locate the risk in the wrong sentence itself. It locates it in belief: risk arises "when users believe false content - often due to the confident nature of the response". That changes what you design. A wrong answer phrased with a citation is believed more, not less. The counter-move is an assistant that is visibly willing to say "not from policy", so that staff learn its confident answers mean something.

Part 2 · covers the hand-off as a product: what it says and what it opens

The hand-off

6 min live

Most teams write the hand-off last, as an error string. It is the opposite of an error: it is the behaviour that makes the board say GO. On rung 4 the assistant hands off 15 of 54 golden questions. That is the share of conversations where staff meet the hand-off, so it deserves the same design care as the answer.

LiveWhat the message says, in three moves3 min▶

It says plainly that it cannot answer from policy. Not "I'm not sure", which invites a rephrase that games the score, and not "please contact HR", which sends staff back to the queue they came from. It shows what it looked at. Naming the closest clause and why it falls short tells the asker the assistant did search, and tells the policy owner later which document nearly answered. It says who has it now. A team name and a ticket number turn a dead end into a queue position.

The test of a good hand-off: the person reading it knows what happens next without asking anyone. The test of a bad one: they open a ticket by hand, retyping the question the assistant already had.

LiveWhat it opens: one ticket, and only one tool3 min▶

The assistant's only action is opening a ticket in the ticketing system. The ticket carries the question, the asker's group, the top three clauses it found with their scores, and the reason it handed off. The helpdesk agent who picks it up starts where the assistant stopped, not from zero. For the restricted questions, the asker group is what lets a manager or HR see a question the asker could not have answered alone.

This page takes the leader side: what the ticket must contain and who receives it. Builder session 6 writes the abstain rule and the ticket payload as code. Assistants that go on to act in other systems are multi-step agents, owned by the AI agents course.

Self-studyWhy satisfaction scores punish a good hand-off3 min▶

On the bench, the constructed thumbs-up rate is 90.7% on rungs 1 to 3 and falls to 81.5% on rung 4, because staff thumb down hand-offs. The rung with zero confident wrong high-risk answers grades worse than the rung that leaks. If your launch dashboard leads with satisfaction, the hand-off will look like the problem, and someone will propose lowering the threshold to fix the score.

Keep the thumbs signal for what it can see: tone, speed, and whether the hand-off message was useful. Read hand-off thumbs separately from answer thumbs, and judge the message by whether the ticket was resolved without a second ticket from the same person. The go/no-go itself stays on the board.

Real world

The thumbs drop at Tidewell (constructed). On the bench, adding the hand-off moves Tidewell's thumbs-up rate from 90.7% to 81.5%, still over its 75% bar. The thumbs signal is constructed to make a point: staff mostly like confident sentences and mostly dislike being told "not from policy", whatever the sentence actually says. OWASP's prevention advice for LLM09 runs the other way: human oversight for critical information. The hand-off is that oversight, delivered as a ticket.

Part 3 · covers choosing the abstain threshold as a product decision

Pick a threshold

5 min live

The hand-off fires when the best clause scores under a line. On the bench the line sits at 3.0. That number is not a law of retrieval; it is a decision Tidewell made, and it has a measured price on both sides. Two questions on either side of it show the price.

LiveThe two questions that price the line3 min▶

G54, the staff party question. No Tidewell policy covers it. Its best match is the annual leave clause at 3.71, which clears the 3.0 line, so rung 4 answers it confidently and wrongly. It is low risk, and the low-risk limit is 20%, so rung 4 still passes at 7.1%. That pass is a policy choice: Tidewell decided one wrong answer about a party is tolerable.

G07, the pay day question. A policy does answer it, but the best clause scores 2.77 and the assistant hands off. Safe, and a miss: a person answers a question the assistant could have. G07 is one of four such misses on the bench, with G17 laptop refresh, G37 carry forward and G38 remote days. They are why correct answers fall from 97.6% to 90.5%.

LiveMove it both ways before you sign it2 min▶

On the bench in Builder session 8, drag "Abstain below" down towards 0 and the assistant behaves like rung 3 again: fewer tickets, and the confident wrong answers come back. Drag it up past 6 and coverage collapses until the board fails on correct answers. Somewhere between, Tidewell picked 3.0. To hand off G54 you would have to raise the line above 3.71, and every question that scores between 3.0 and your new line would hand off with it; the bench prints which. To answer G07 you would have to drop below 2.77, and every question in that band would be answered.

The decision belongs to the person who owns the go/no-go, written down with the numbers that justified it. Risk tiers in depth, and how a tier changes the tolerance, live in the agent launch course's risk tiers page; this page takes the single threshold and its trade.

Self-studyThe line is tied to this corpus, not portable2 min▶

A BM25 score depends on the clause lengths, the vocabulary and how many clauses share a word. Tidewell's 3.0 means something on 49 clause versions with an average length of 21.76 tokens; it means nothing on your corpus, or on Tidewell's after a large policy rewrite. Re-pick the line whenever the store changes shape, and re-run the golden questions when you do. Scoring methods beyond hit rate and MRR, and grading answer quality with a model, belong to the AI evals course.

Real world

What each hand-off costs Tidewell (constructed). Every hand-off is a helpdesk ticket, priced by assumption at 12 minutes and SGD 45 an hour, so SGD 9 each. At rung 4 the hand-off tickets come to SGD 6,000 a month on an assumed 2,400 questions, against a model cost of SGD 0.25 to 8.53. Raising the line is cheap in model terms and expensive in tickets. Session 5 builds that sheet; tonight, just know that the threshold is also a budget line.

Build-along

Tidewell's hand-off message and threshold note

★ 20 min · everyone builds

You write three short documents: what staff read, what the ticket carries, and the paragraph that records the threshold decision. Use your own assistant if you have one in pilot; otherwise write them for Tidewell with the numbers on this page. Tidewell is constructed, and your numbers will differ.

List the questions your assistant should not answer. Two kinds: restricted (a clause answers it, but this asker may not read it) and no policy at all. Tidewell's golden set has six of each. Take yours from session 1's N tickets and session 3's access matrix. Give each a risk tier: high, medium or low.

Write the message staff read. Three moves, plain words, no apology spiral. Read it aloud; if it sounds like an error page, rewrite it.

★ Block 1 · the hand-off message, Tidewell styleI can't answer that from Tidewell's policies. The closest I found was [clause title, document], but it covers [what it covers], not your question. I've opened ticket [number] with [owning team], including your question and what I found. You'll hear from them in [service time]. You don't need to raise it again.

Write the ticket template. Every field is something the agent who answers would otherwise ask for. If a field would never change what they do, drop it.

★ Block 2 · the ticket the assistant opensTitle: Assistant hand-off: [first 60 characters of the question] Asker group: [all staff | managers | HR only | finance only | IT only] Question: [verbatim] Top clauses: [key v.version, title, score] x 3 Why: top score [2.77] under the line [3.0] Route to: [owner team of the top clause, else the helpdesk] Feedback: after answering, mark: policy exists | policy missing | restricted

Write the threshold decision note. One paragraph, numbers from the bench or your own run, and a name. A threshold with no owner drifts the first time someone dislikes the thumbs-up rate.

★ Block 3 · the threshold decision noteDecision: the assistant hands off when its best clause scores below 3.0. Chosen by: [name, role], on [date], on the constructed Tidewell golden set (54 questions). At 3.0: right hand-offs 91.7%, correct answers 90.5%, confident wrong 0.0% high, 0.0% medium, 7.1% low risk. Board: GO. What it misses: 4 answerable questions are handed off (G07, G17, G37, G38). What slips past: G54 (staff party, low risk) is answered wrongly at 3.71. Why not higher: every question between 3.0 and the new line would hand off too. Why not lower: the assistant drifts back towards rung 3, which is NO-GO. Review: whenever the policy store changes shape, and before any change to the line.

Read all three to the helpdesk lead who will receive the tickets. Ask one question: "If fifteen of every fifty-four questions arrived like this, would your team answer faster or slower than today?" Their answer goes into session 5's cost sheet.

✗ The hand-off written as an error message Sorry, I couldn't find an answer to your question. Please try rephrasing, or contact HR, IT or Finance directly.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Leader session 4 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Rung 3 answers 41 of 42 answerable questions correctly (97.6%). Why does the board still say NO-GO?

Rung 3 has 0 leaks and 0 stale citations, so A and B are fixed already, and 97.6% is far above 70%. What it lacks is a rule for "the best match is not good enough", so the restricted and no-policy questions get confident answers.

2 · After adding the hand-off, Tidewell's thumbs-up rate falls from 90.7% to 81.5%. A stakeholder proposes lowering the threshold to win the thumbs back. What do you say?

Lowering the line moves the assistant back towards rung 3, which grades better on thumbs and fails the board. Thumbs are a real signal for tone and hand-off quality, so keep them (not C), but they cannot see a confident wrong answer, so they cannot be the verdict.

3 · G54, the staff party question, is answered wrongly at a top score of 3.71. Someone wants to raise the line from 3.0 to 4.0 to catch it. What must happen before that ships?

The line is a trade: catching G54 also hands off whatever else scores in that band, and coverage can fall below the 70% bar. A ignores that cost. B misreads the mechanism: the line sits on retrieval scores, not on the model. C would make the assistant cite a party rule inside a leave policy.

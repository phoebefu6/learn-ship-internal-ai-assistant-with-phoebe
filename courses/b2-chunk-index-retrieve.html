Part 0

Where retrieval sits in the build

Every answer the assistant gives starts here: a question goes in, a ranked list of clauses comes out, and everything later in the pipeline, the permission filter, the version filter, the citation check and the hand-off, works on that list. If the right clause is not in it, no amount of careful generation brings it back. So tonight is about one retriever you can read line by line. Tidewell Freight and every clause, question and score shown are constructed; the formula is the published one.

Part 1 · covers clause-level chunks and the metadata they carry

One clause, one chunk

6 min live

A chunk is whatever the retriever ranks and the answer cites. For policy text the authors have already chosen it for you: the numbered clause. Cut anywhere else and you get chunks with no single key to cite, no single owner to fix them and no single access group to filter on.

LiveCut on the clause boundary3 min▶

Tidewell's 16 documents become 49 chunks, one per clause version, averaging 21.76 tokens once stopwords are removed. No overlap, no sliding window, no clause split across two chunks. Three reasons, each one a later session:

  • Citation. Session 4 checks that the cited key is in the retrieved set and current. A chunk that straddles two clauses has two keys and cannot pass or fail that check honestly.
  • Permission. Session 5 filters on the clause's access group. A window that joins an all-staff clause to a managers-only clause has no single group to filter on.
  • Version. Session 7 drops superseded versions. A clause that changed carries its own flag; half of a clause does not.
LiveWhat travels into the index with the text2 min▶

BM25 ranks only the title and text: tokenize(title + " " + text). But every indexed chunk keeps a pointer back to its full clause record, with all eight fields from session 1. That is what lets the permission and version filters run on the candidate list before the top three are chosen, instead of after an answer has already been written from the wrong clause.

Superseded versions are indexed too. That is deliberate: the index mirrors the store, and the version filter, not deletion, keeps old rules out of answers. You will see in Part 2 what that costs when the filter is missing.

Self-studyWhen the documents are not written in clauses2 min▶

Meeting notes, wiki pages and long procedures have no numbered clauses, and then chunk size, overlap and structure-aware splitting become real design choices with measurable trade-offs. Which side this track takes: clause-level chunks and one lexical retriever, BM25, because every score can be checked by hand. Chunking strategies, embeddings and vector databases belong to the RAG course's chunking session; start there when your corpus is not policy text.

Real world

The index is the part you can change on a Tuesday. Lewis and colleagues (2020), the paper that named retrieval-augmented generation, showed that the knowledge in such a system "can be easily updated at test time" by replacing the retrieved index, without retraining the model. At clause granularity that becomes routine work: when finance changes the meals allowance, one chunk is added, one is flagged, and the next question retrieves the new rule.

Part 2 · covers BM25 term weights and IDF, eq. 3.15 and eq. 3.3

BM25 by hand

6 min live

BM25 gives each clause a score for a question by adding one weight per query word the clause contains. Each weight grows with how often the word appears in the clause, shrinks if the clause is long, and is multiplied by how rare the word is across the whole store. Two equations, both from Robertson and Zaragoza (2009). Here they are on a real Tidewell question.

LiveThe two equations, in words2 min▶

The rarity weight, eq. 3.3 (p.349). With no relevance information, the weight of a term is log((N - n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5)), where N is the number of clauses and n the number that contain the term. The paper calls it "a close approximation to classical idf". "diem" appears in 1 of Tidewell's 49 clause versions, so it weighs 3.48; "per" appears in 3, so it weighs 2.59.

The term weight, eq. 3.15 (p.360). tf / (k1 x ((1 - b) + b x dl/avdl) + tf) x idf. tf is how often the term appears in this clause, dl the clause's length, avdl the average length. The fraction saturates: the fifth "per" adds far less than the first. b controls how hard long clauses are pulled down: b = 1 is full length normalisation, b = 0 switches it off. A clause's score is the sum over the query's distinct terms.

The bench uses exactly these two, with k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75. Many library variants multiply by (k1 + 1) on top; the paper notes that factor "does not affect the ranking", so it is left out.

LiveCompute three clauses in plain Python, and run it4 min▶

The inputs are counted from Tidewell's constructed store: N and avdl for the whole index, how many clauses contain each query term, and each clause's length and term counts. Save as by_hand.py in an empty folder.

★ by_hand.py · three clauses, one questionimport math K1, B = 1.2, 0.75 N, AVDL = 49, 21.76 # Tidewell's store: 49 clause versions, average length in tokens DF = {"per": 3, "diem": 1, "meal": 2, "travelling": 2} # clauses containing each term query = ["per", "diem", "meal", "travelling"] clauses = { # dl = clause length in tokens, tf = term counts "FIN-EXP-2@v4 Per diem (superseded)": (20, {"per": 5, "diem": 3, "meal": 1, "travelling": 1}), "FIN-EXP-2@v5 Meals when travelling": (21, {"meal": 2, "travelling": 2}), "HR-WFH-1@v1 Remote work (superseded)": (20, {"per": 1}), } def idf(t): # eq. 3.3 n = DF[t] return math.log((N - n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5)) def weight(tf, dl, t): # eq. 3.15 return tf / (K1 * ((1 - B) + B * dl / AVDL) + tf) * idf(t) for name, (dl, tf) in clauses.items(): parts = {t: weight(tf[t], dl, t) for t in query if tf.get(t)} total = sum(parts.values()) print(f"{total:5.2f} {name}") for t, w in parts.items(): print(f" {t:<11} tf={tf[t]} idf={idf(t):.2f} adds {w:.2f}")

python3 by_hand.py prints exactly this, and the three totals match what the bench in session 8 ranks for G41:

★ Output 7.41 FIN-EXP-2@v4 Per diem (superseded) per tf=5 idf=2.59 adds 2.11 diem tf=3 idf=3.48 adds 2.53 meal tf=1 idf=2.94 adds 1.38 travelling tf=1 idf=2.94 adds 1.38 3.72 FIN-EXP-2@v5 Meals when travelling meal tf=2 idf=2.94 adds 1.86 travelling tf=2 idf=2.94 adds 1.86 1.22 HR-WFH-1@v1 Remote work (superseded) per tf=1 idf=2.59 adds 1.22

Read the first block slowly. Five "per"s add only 2.11, less than three "diem"s: that is saturation. And the clause that wins is the one HR replaced. BM25 is doing its job; it has no idea what superseded means. That is session 7's filter.

Self-studyChoosing k1 and b2 min▶

Robertson and Zaragoza write that "values such as 0.5 < b < 0.8 and 1.2 < k1 < 2 are reasonably good in many circumstances", and add that the optimal values depend on the documents and the queries; the model itself gives no guidance on setting them. Tidewell's k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75 sit inside that range. Treat them as a starting point you tune against your own golden questions from session 3, never as a law.

Try it: change B to 0 in by_hand.py and rerun. Length stops mattering. Then try 1. Write down which clause's score moved most, and why its length explains it.

Real world

A formula with no right answer for its own knobs. The 2009 survey that set out BM25 in this form, Robertson and Zaragoza in Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval, is candid that the parameters must be tuned on the collection in hand. That is a feature for a policy assistant: when a score surprises you, every input is a count you can check, and every knob is a number you chose and can defend.

Part 3 · covers top-k retrieval, hit rate, MRR and lexical gaps

Top-3 retrieval

5 min live

The retriever hands the top three clauses to the rest of the pipeline. Two numbers say how well it does that across the 42 answerable golden questions: top-3 hit rate, the share where the right current clause is somewhere in the three, and MRR, which also cares whether it came first.

LiveHit rate and MRR, computed2 min▶

For each answerable question, find the rank of the right current clause in the top three: 1, 2, 3 or absent. Hit rate counts how often it is present. MRR averages 1/rank, scoring an absence as 0. On the bench, hit rate is 100.0% on every rung: the right clause is always somewhere in the three. MRR is not:

Bench rungRanked 1st / 2nd / 3rdSum of 1/rankMRR (of 42)
1 and 2, no version filter35 / 6 / 135 + 3 + 0.3330.913
3 and 4, version filter on41 / 1 / 041 + 0.50.988

The seven questions that drop below first at rungs 1 and 2 are the ones from Part 2's story: six lose first place to a superseded clause worded the way staff still ask, and one, G07, loses to the overtime clause. The version filter fixes the six. It cannot fix G07, because G07's problem is a word, not a version.

LiveLexical gaps: when the words do not meet2 min▶

BM25 matches tokens, not meanings. Tidewell's tokenizer lower-cases, drops stopwords and a trailing "s", and does no stemming, so "month's" becomes "month" but "monthly" stays "monthly". The pay day clause answers G07 perfectly and loses first place to overtime by a margin you cannot see at two decimals. The same gap shows up whenever staff and policy authors use different words for one thing: "grandmother" against "immediate family member", "per diem" against "daily allowance".

Top-3 hides most of this, because the right clause is still in the list. It stops hiding when session 6 adds a confidence threshold on the top score, and when session 4 cites only the first clause: on the bench's rung 4, G07 is one of the four questions handed off that a person did not need to see.

Self-studyWhat closes a lexical gap, and whose job it is1 min▶

Three levers, cheapest first. Fix the text: Tidewell's current leave clause opens "Annual leave, formerly called holiday entitlement", which bridges the vocabulary inside the store. Fix the tokens: stemming or a synonym list, measured against your golden set. Fix the retriever: add dense retrieval alongside BM25, or a reranker after it. The last two are the RAG course's ground, in hybrid search with metadata and reranking; metric design beyond hit rate and MRR is the AI evals course's, in retrieval metrics. This track stays with one lexical retriever so every later failure can be traced to a count.

Real world

Retrieval only helps if it retrieves the right thing. Singapore's Model AI Governance Framework for Generative AI (IMDA and AI Verify Foundation, 2024) names RAG among techniques "commonly used to reduce hallucinations and improve accuracy". The mechanism is the ranked list you built tonight. When an old clause ranks first, as the per diem one does without a version filter, retrieval supplies a confident, well-cited, out-of-date answer, which is why the list is measured before anything is generated from it.

Build-along

A forty-line BM25 over six clauses

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Plain Python 3, standard library only. Six constructed Tidewell clauses, shortened from the store: two versions of the leave rule, two of the meals rule, pay day and overtime. You build the tokenizer, the index and the scorer, ask three questions, and meet one trap that a small index sets and a big one hides. Run it from an empty scratch folder.

Write the stopwords and six clauses. The texts are shortened from Tidewell's constructed store; keep the old versions in, exactly as the real index does.

★ bm25.py · part 1, the clauses (constructed)import math, re STOP = set("a an the and or of to in on for is are be by with at as it from i my do does how what when which can many".split()) CLAUSES = [ ("HR-LV-1@v3", "Annual leave. Full-time staff earn 20 days of annual leave each calendar year."), ("HR-LV-1@v2", "Holiday entitlement. Every employee receives 18 days holiday entitlement each year."), ("HR-PAY-1@v4", "Pay day. Salary is paid monthly on the 25th, or the previous working day."), ("HR-PAY-3@v4", "Overtime. Overtime is paid at 1.5 times the hourly rate with next month's salary."), ("FIN-EXP-2@v5", "Meals when travelling. A daily allowance of SGD 60 in Singapore, SGD 90 abroad."), ("FIN-EXP-2@v4", "Per diem. Per diem for meals when travelling: SGD 50 per day in Singapore."), ]

Tokenize and build the index. The same rules as the bench: lower case, split on anything not a letter or digit, drop stopwords, drop a trailing "s" from words over three letters unless it ends "ss". Then count each clause's length and, for every term, how many clauses contain it.

★ bm25.py · part 2, tokenizer and indexdef tokenize(text): out = [] for t in re.split(r"[^a-z0-9]+", text.lower()): if t and t not in STOP: out.append(t[:-1] if len(t) > 3 and t.endswith("s") and not t.endswith("ss") else t) return out docs = [tokenize(text) for _, text in CLAUSES] N = len(docs) AVDL = sum(len(d) for d in docs) / N DF = {} for d in docs: for t in set(d): DF[t] = DF.get(t, 0) + 1

Score and rank. One loop over the question's distinct terms, eq. 3.3 for the rarity weight, eq. 3.15 for the term weight. Keep the top three with a score above zero.

★ bm25.py · part 3, BM25 and top-3def bm25(query, d, k1=1.2, b=0.75): score = 0.0 for t in set(tokenize(query)): tf = d.count(t) if tf: idf = math.log((N - DF[t] + 0.5) / (DF[t] + 0.5)) score += tf / (k1 * ((1 - b) + b * len(d) / AVDL) + tf) * idf return score def top3(query): ranked = sorted(((bm25(query, d), key) for d, (key, _) in zip(docs, CLAUSES)), reverse=True) return [(key, round(s, 2)) for s, key in ranked[:3] if s > 0] print(f"N={N} avdl={AVDL:.2f} vocabulary={len(DF)}") for q in ["What is the per diem for meals when travelling?", "How many days of annual leave do I get?", "Which day is salary paid this month?"]: print(q, "->", top3(q))

Run it and read the three lines. python3 bm25.py prints exactly this. The per diem question picks the superseded clause again, as in Part 2. The salary question puts overtime first again, as on the bench. And the leave question returns only one clause: where did the old holiday version go?

★ OutputN=6 avdl=11.00 vocabulary=44 What is the per diem for meals when travelling? -> [('FIN-EXP-2@v4', 2.27), ('FIN-EXP-2@v5', 0.55)] How many days of annual leave do I get? -> [('HR-LV-1@v3', 1.3)] Which day is salary paid this month? -> [('HR-PAY-3@v4', 1.08), ('HR-PAY-1@v4', 0.19)]

Find the trap. Append three lines that print each term's clause count and idf. "day" is in four of the six clauses, more than half, so eq. 3.3 goes negative and every "day" subtracts from the score. The old holiday clause matched only "day", so it scored below zero and was dropped; the pay day clause fell to 0.19 for the same reason. This is our own arithmetic, not a claim from the paper. In Tidewell's 49 clauses no term is in more than half, so the bench never meets it; a small or repetitive index will.

★ bm25.py · append, then run againfor t in ["day", "salary", "diem"]: print(t, DF[t], round(math.log((N - DF[t] + 0.5) / (DF[t] + 0.5)), 2)) # prints (after the three lines above): # day 4 -0.59 # salary 2 0.59 # diem 1 1.3

Point it at your own text. Replace the six clauses with twenty from your own policies, one per clause, and ask five questions your staff really ask. Your numbers will differ. Note every question where the right clause is not first, and whether the cause is an old version, a common word or a gap between the asker's words and the author's.

Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and Builder session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · For "What is the per diem for meals when travelling?", the superseded FIN-EXP-2 v4 scores 7.41 and the current v5 scores 3.72. Why?

The per-term breakdown shows it: "diem" alone adds 2.53 to v4 and nothing to v5. The two clauses are 20 and 21 tokens, so length is not the story. Keeping old versions out of the answer is the version filter's job in session 7.

2 · On the bench, top-3 hit rate is 100.0% at rung 1 but MRR is 0.913. What does that combination tell you?

35 questions rank it first, 6 second and 1 third: (35 + 3 + 0.333) / 42 = 0.913. Nothing is missed, so hit rate stays at 100.0%; MRR sees the order. The version filter lifts it to 0.988.

3 · In the six-clause build-along, "day" gets an idf of -0.59 and the old holiday clause disappears from the leave question's results. What happened?

With N = 6 and n = 4 the ratio is 2.5 / 4.5, and its log is negative. The build-along has no version filter, and "days" became "day", not a stopword. In Tidewell's 49 clauses no term is that common, so the bench never meets it.
